There is a pattern I see all over the South Shore, and it always makes me smile before it makes me curious. A business that has been around for decades is now in its second or third generation. The son or daughter who grew up sweeping the floor is running the place. They have added services, picked up new customers, maybe expanded the shop. The business is genuinely thriving.

And then you look at the website, and it stopped in time. The copyright line at the bottom reads a year that is five or six years gone. The design belongs to an older platform. The photos are of a storefront that has since been renovated. The site is describing a business that no longer quite exists, because the business kept growing and the website stayed put.

I want to be clear that this is not a criticism. It is one of the most understandable things in the world. When you are actually running a business, the website is the last thing you think about, right up until a new customer mentions they almost did not call because the site looked closed. The frozen site is a side effect of being busy, not of being careless.

## Why the handoff is the moment

Here is the part I find genuinely interesting. A generational handoff is not just a change of ownership on paper. The new operators are building their own reputation. They have their own way of doing the work, their own priorities, often their own ideas about where the business goes next. They are, in a real sense, introducing themselves to the community all over again, even to customers who have been coming for years.

A website that froze a generation ago works against that introduction. It tells the story of the business the way it was told before the handoff. It undersells people who are doing good work right now. When a prospective customer looks the business up, which nearly all of them do before they call or walk in, the site is the first impression. If that impression is dated, it quietly suggests the business itself might be dated, even when the opposite is true.

So the handoff is not just an occasion when the website *could* be refreshed. It is arguably the best occasion there is. The new operator has a clear reason to make the site their own, and a clean story to tell: here is who we are now, here is what we do, here is what has changed and what has stayed the same. That is a much easier and more honest project than a refresh with no clear reason behind it.

## What a refresh actually looks like

It is usually smaller than people fear. A refresh does not mean tearing everything down or reinventing the brand that took decades to earn. Often it means current photos, an updated description of the services the business actually offers today, the new operator's name and voice somewhere on the page, working contact information, and a design that loads well on a phone. The goal is simply to have the website tell the truth about the business as it is now.

The years of trust the family built are an asset, not a thing to discard. A good refresh carries that history forward. It keeps what earned the loyalty and updates what has quietly gone stale. Customers who have been coming for thirty years should still recognize the place, and someone finding it for the first time should see a business that is clearly open, current, and proud of its work.

## A small takeaway

If you have recently taken over a family business, or you are watching the next generation step into one, it is worth taking two minutes to look at the website the way a first-time customer would. Read the copyright year. Check whether the photos match the place today. Ask whether the site sounds like the business you are running now.

If the answer gives you pause, there is no rush and no alarm. It just means the website has a little catching up to do with a business that kept moving. The handoff is a natural, low-stress moment to let it catch up. I am always glad to talk it through if that would help.
