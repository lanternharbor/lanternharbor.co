A while back I started paying attention to how long the homepages of South Shore businesses take to show up on my phone. Not the fancy stuff, just the first thing you see when the page opens. On a few of them, I'd tap the link and then sit there watching a blank or half-loaded screen for what felt like a long time before anything appeared. When I looked closer, the pattern was usually the same: a big video set to play automatically across the top of the page.

I want to walk through why that happens, because it's a common setup, it's easy to miss, and it's usually fixable without rebuilding your site.

## Why the video is the slow part

A background video that autoplays on your homepage is often a large file. Fifteen to twenty-five megabytes is normal for a nice-looking clip of a few seconds. That whole file generally has to travel to the visitor's phone before the video can start playing smoothly.

On a strong home Wi-Fi connection, that download is quick and you'd never notice. But most of the people finding your business are not on your office Wi-Fi. They're on a phone, on cell data, often in a car or walking somewhere or standing in a parking lot. On that kind of connection, a twenty-megabyte file can take many seconds to arrive. And during those seconds, the top of your page can look empty or unfinished.

Here's the part that matters: a lot of visitors won't wait. They tapped your link, saw a blank screen, assumed something was wrong, and went back to their search results to try the next business. You never see those people. They don't fill out a form or call. They just quietly leave, and the video that was meant to impress them is the reason they never saw anything at all.

## A still photo does almost the same job

The thing the video is usually trying to do is set a mood. Show the harbor, the shop, the team, the finished work. A single high-quality photo of that same scene does most of that job, and it commonly loads in under a second even on a phone.

You lose the motion, yes. But you gain every visitor who was about to give up. For most small businesses that's a good trade, because the goal of the homepage isn't to win a design award. It's to keep someone on the page long enough to read what you do and get in touch.

If the motion really matters to you, there are middle options too. You can compress the video down to a fraction of its size, show a photo first and let the video load quietly behind it, or move the video further down the page where it isn't the first thing that has to load. None of those require starting over.

## How to check your own site

You can test this yourself in a couple of minutes. Turn off Wi-Fi on your phone so you're on cell data, the way most visitors are. Then open your homepage as if you'd never seen it. Count how long it takes before the top of the page actually looks finished. If it's more than two or three seconds, that's worth looking into.

If you want to be more precise, free tools like Google's PageSpeed Insights will load your page and tell you how heavy it is and how long it takes. You don't need to understand every number. Just look at whether the page is slow and whether a big media file is the reason.

## The takeaway

A heavy homepage doesn't announce itself. Nothing breaks. You just get a little less of what you were hoping for, and it's hard to connect that to a video you're proud of. So it's worth a quick look.
