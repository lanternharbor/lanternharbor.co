If you have talked to a contractor lately, you have probably heard both words used almost interchangeably: an addition and an ADU. They sound similar, and they can even cost about the same to build. But they solve different problems, and the choice between them shapes how you use your home for the next twenty years. I have been having this conversation with a few South Shore homeowners, so I wanted to lay out the trade-offs plainly.

## The core difference

An addition expands your existing home. You add a bedroom, a bigger kitchen, a first-floor suite. It is still one household under one roof, sharing one front door and one set of utilities.

An ADU, an accessory dwelling unit, is a separate small home on your property. It has its own entrance, its own kitchen, and its own bathroom. It can be attached to your house, sit above a garage, or stand on its own in the yard. Because it is a complete living space, someone can live there fully independently.

That independence is the whole point, and it is where the two paths split.

## What each one is good for

Choose an **addition** when your household is staying one household. You want more room for the family you already have, a home office, a primary suite on the main floor so you can age in place, or space for a child who will eventually move out. An addition keeps everything connected, which is exactly what you want when the goal is simply more room.

Choose an **ADU** when you want a separate living space. That usually means one of two things. Either you want to house family with some independence, such as a parent who wants their own kitchen and door but wants to be close by, or an adult child getting started. Or you want the option to rent the space and bring in income. An addition cannot do either of those, because it is part of your home rather than a home of its own.

## The money and paperwork side

The financing tends to run differently. An addition is usually paid for the conventional way most people already know: a home equity line, a cash-out refinance, or savings. It is familiar territory for lenders because it is just an improvement to your existing house.

An ADU has an option an addition does not. Massachusetts now allows ADUs of up to 900 square feet by right in single-family zones, which means you are not asking your town for special permission the way you often had to before. Alongside that, there is a state ADU loan program aimed specifically at helping homeowners build one. So while an ADU is a bigger undertaking, being a full dwelling with its own systems, there is a financing path built around it that did not exist a few years ago.

The trade-off is real on both sides. The addition is simpler to build and simpler to finance, and you stay one household. The ADU is more to build and manage, but it opens doors the addition keeps closed: rental income, or a family member living close with real independence.

## A quick way to decide

Here is the question I find cuts through it fastest. Picture the space five years from now. Is someone living in it separately from you, with their own door and their own kitchen? If yes, you are describing an ADU, and it is worth looking at the by-right rules and the state loan before you scope anything. If the honest answer is that it is just more room for your own household, an addition is almost certainly the simpler and cheaper way to get there.

Neither one is the "right" answer in the abstract. They are answers to different questions, and the trouble usually starts when a project gets built as one when the family actually needed the other.
