If you have looked into adding a small backyard cottage or in-law unit lately, you have probably seen the good news: Massachusetts made accessory dwelling units by-right in early 2025. Under Chapter 150 of the Acts of 2024, a homeowner can add an ADU of up to 900 square feet without going through a special permit fight at town hall. That change opened a real door for a lot of families on the South Shore who want a place for an aging parent, a returning adult child, or some rental income.

But the law is rarely the part that stops these projects. On the South Shore, the question that actually decides whether an ADU pencils out is usually septic.

## Why septic, not zoning, is the real gate

A lot of older homes here are not on town sewer. They run on a private Title 5 septic system, and that system was designed and sized for the house that sits over it. The state rates septic capacity partly by the number of bedrooms it is built to serve. When you add a second dwelling with its own kitchen and bathroom, you are adding demand the original system may not have been sized to carry.

That can mean one of a few things. Your existing system might have enough headroom already. It might need to be upgraded. Or the project might call for a whole new system design. Those outcomes sit very far apart on both cost and timeline, and that is the part people often do not see coming. The zoning approval feels like the finish line, when the septic answer is what quietly sets the budget.

I want to be clear that this is not a reason to give up on the idea. It is a reason to ask the question early, before you have paid anyone to draw plans around an assumption that may not hold.

## What to check before you draw plans

The good part is that almost all of this is knowable upfront. You are not gambling. A few things worth lining up before you get emotionally or financially invested in a design:

- **A current Title 5 inspection.** If your system has not been inspected recently, that report tells you its condition and what it is rated to handle. It is the natural starting point.
- **System capacity versus what the ADU adds.** Compare what your system was designed for against the bedroom count and load the new unit would introduce. This is where you learn whether you have headroom or a gap.
- **Soil and perc conditions.** If a new or expanded system is on the table, the soil on your lot decides what is even possible and how much it costs. A perc test answers that.
- **Whether town sewer is available.** Some parcels sit within reach of a sewer connection even if the house is currently on septic. If that option exists, it can change the whole calculation, so it is worth confirming with your town.

None of these steps require you to commit to building. They just replace a guess with a number. A septic professional and your town's health or building department can usually get you most of the way to an answer without much expense.

## The point is no surprises

The reason I keep coming back to this is simple. The worst version of an ADU project is the one where someone falls in love with a design, lines up a contractor, and only then finds out the septic system needs to be replaced. That is an expensive surprise arriving at the worst possible moment.

The better version starts with the septic question, gets a clear answer, and then designs to what the site can actually support. Same project, much less stress, and a budget you can trust.

If you are weighing an ADU on the South Shore and are not sure how to read your own septic situation, that is exactly the kind of thing worth sorting out before the drawings start. It is usually a short conversation and a couple of records, and it tells you whether you are looking at a straightforward add or something bigger. Either way, you would rather know now.
