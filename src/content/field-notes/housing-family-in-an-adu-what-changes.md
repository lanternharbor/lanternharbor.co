A lot of the ADU conversations I have on the South Shore start the same way. Someone's mom is rattling around a big house an hour away and everyone would rest easier if she were close. Or a grown kid is back home after school, saving to launch, and the current arrangement is starting to feel tight for everyone. An accessory dwelling unit, a second small home on the same lot, is often the answer people land on. It keeps family near without asking anyone to give up their own front door.

That last part is the whole idea, and it's also where the real decisions live. Building the unit is a construction project. Living next to family in it is a housing decision, and those play out day to day for years. Here's what I've seen actually change once people move in.

## Privacy is the thing that makes it work

The reason an ADU beats a spare bedroom is that everyone keeps a life of their own. That only holds if the space is genuinely separate. A separate entrance matters more than square footage. Being able to come and go without walking through someone else's kitchen is what lets a parent stay independent and lets your household stay yours.

It's worth walking the lot before you fall in love with a floor plan. Where do the two front doors face? Can each home have a bit of outdoor space that feels private? Do windows look straight into each other? These sound like small design questions, but they set the tone for how it feels to live there. The families who are happiest a year in are usually the ones who protected everyone's separateness on purpose, not by accident.

## Shared or separate utilities is a real fork

Early on you'll decide how the ADU gets its heat, power, and water. Some households run everything off the main house and keep it simple. Others put the ADU on its own meters so costs are clean and independent.

There's no single right answer, but the choice has a long tail. Shared utilities are cheaper and faster up front and fine when it's your mom. Separate utilities cost more to set up and make far more sense if you might ever rent the unit, or if you just want clear lines about who pays for what. It's easier to decide this deliberately now than to untangle it later, so it's a good early conversation with your builder.

## What "by-right" does and doesn't mean

You'll hear "by-right" a lot around ADUs in Massachusetts right now, and it's genuinely good news. In plain terms, it means a qualifying ADU is generally allowed without having to plead your case for special permission, which used to be a common roadblock.

Here's the part people miss: by-right is not the same as no rules. You still work within a set of standards, and your specific lot, town, and plans all shape what's actually allowed. So treat "by-right" as "this is a real, permitted path" rather than "anything goes." The details are exactly the kind of thing your town's building or planning department is there to walk you through, and it's smart to have that conversation early rather than after the drawings are done.

## It's a family arrangement first

The quiet truth is that most of what determines whether an ADU works isn't in the permit. It's the conversations. Who handles the yard. How drop-in visits work. What happens if the parent's needs change, or the kid moves on and you're thinking about a tenant. None of that needs to be solved on day one, but it helps enormously to have talked about it out loud before the concrete is poured.

## A quiet takeaway

If an ADU is on your mind, you don't have to have it all figured out to take a first step. Two conversations tend to clear the fog fast: one with your town about what your lot allows, and one with a builder or another professional about how to lay it out and connect it. Everything above is general and practical, not legal or tax advice, so lean on those folks for your specifics.

An ADU can be one of the warmest things a family does, keeping people close while everyone keeps their own life. Getting the boring parts right early is what makes the good part last.
