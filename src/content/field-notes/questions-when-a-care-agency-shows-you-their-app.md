If you are helping a parent stay at home, at some point an agency will sit across from you and open an app. A family portal on a tablet, or a caregiver app on a phone. It usually looks polished, and the person showing it clearly wants you to feel reassured.

That is a good moment, not a stressful one. You do not need to know anything about software to ask the right things. A demo is your chance to picture what the next year actually feels like: the day-to-day of checking in on someone you love from your own kitchen. Here are four questions I would ask, and why each one matters.

## Can I see the visit notes and the schedule?

Ask them to show you, on the screen, what you would see as a family member. Not the sales view, the real one. Can you open this week's schedule and know who is coming and when? After a visit, can you read a short note about how your parent did, what was eaten, whether a walk happened, anything that felt off?

The point is not that longer notes are better. The point is that you can see enough to feel connected on a Tuesday when you are stuck at work. Some families want a lot of detail, others just want to know the visit happened. Ask which one this app gives you, and whether you can get a text or email when something changes.

## Who can see my parent's information, and how is it kept private?

This is a fair question to ask out loud, and a good agency will welcome it. Your parent's health details, address, and daily routine are sitting in this system. It is reasonable to ask who has access. Just the assigned caregivers and the office? Can family members be added or removed by you? If a caregiver leaves the agency, do they lose access?

You are not accusing anyone of anything. You are simply making sure the circle around your parent is one you understand. Trust your gut on the answer. A clear, calm explanation is a good sign. A vague one is worth noting.

## What happens to the records if I switch agencies?

Nobody plans to switch, but situations change. A parent moves, needs a different level of care, or the fit just is not right. Before you are in that moment, ask what happens to everything stored in the app if you leave.

Can you get a copy of the care history and notes? In what form? This tells you two useful things. First, whether you would walk away empty-handed or with a record you can hand to the next provider or a doctor. Second, and more quietly, it tells you how the agency thinks about your family. An agency comfortable answering this is one that expects to earn your stay rather than trap it.

## Is there a real person to call when the app does not help?

This may be the most important one. Apps are wonderful until the night they are not. The schedule shows the wrong caregiver, a note has something worrying in it, or your parent needs something the app has no button for.

Ask who you call, whether that line is answered by a person, and what the hours are. Ask what happens at 9pm on a Sunday. The app is a convenience layered on top of the care. The care itself still runs on people, and you want to know those people are reachable when it counts.

## The takeaway

A good app should make you feel more in touch with your parent's care, not more managed by a company. You do not have to decide anything during the demo. Ask your four questions, notice how it feels to hear the answers, and give yourself a day to sit with it.
