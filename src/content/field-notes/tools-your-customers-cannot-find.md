A while back I was helping a South Shore owner think through his website, and he mentioned, almost in passing, that clients could upload documents through a portal he paid for every month. I asked where it lived on the site. He clicked around for a bit, then found it three levels deep, under a menu, past the fold, in a spot no first-time visitor would ever reach. He was paying for a good tool. His customers just could not find it.

That situation is more common than you might think, and it is one of the easier wins I come across.

## The tool is usually already there

Most owners I talk to are not missing software. Over the years they have signed up for the useful pieces one at a time. A client login or upload portal. An online payment link so people can settle an invoice without mailing a check. A booking or scheduling system so the phone rings a little less. Each one solves a real problem, and each one costs money every month whether or not anyone uses it.

The trouble is rarely the tool itself. The trouble is that the link to it is hidden. It sits below the fold, where visitors have to scroll to reach it. Or it is tucked inside a footer menu next to the privacy policy. Or, most often, it only exists in an email you sent one customer six months ago, and everyone else has no idea it is an option.

So people call instead. They ask how to pay. They ask if they can send a file. They ask whether you have any openings next Tuesday. You answer the same questions over and over, and the software that was supposed to answer them for you sits quietly in the background, paid for and unused.

## What surfacing it actually means

The fix here is not new technology. It is putting what you already own where a busy person can see it at a glance.

Think about the handful of actions you want a customer to be able to take the moment they land on your site. For a lot of businesses that list is short: **Log in. Upload. Pay. Book.** Those are verbs, and they belong somewhere obvious, near the top, in plain words, on both the phone and the desktop version of your site.

A few things I look for when I do this:

- The link uses the word for the action, not the name of the vendor. "Pay your invoice" reads better than the brand name of your payment processor, which most customers will not recognize.
- It appears near the top, above the fold, so no one has to scroll or hunt to find it.
- It works on a phone, because that is where most people will tap it.
- There is one clear path for each action, not three competing buttons that leave people unsure which one is real.

None of that requires buying anything. In most cases the tool, the account, and the link already exist. You are just moving the door to the front of the building instead of leaving it around the back.

## Why this one is worth doing first

When money is tight, the instinct is to look for the next tool that will fix things. This is the opposite move. It gets more value out of software you are already paying for, at no new cost, and it usually takes an afternoon rather than a project.

It also tends to pay off in two directions at once. Customers get a smoother experience and stop calling to ask how to do the basics. And you stop paying for a portal or a payment link that nobody uses, which is close to the definition of a wasted subscription.

## A small thing to check this week

Next time you have a few minutes, pull up your own website on your phone the way a customer would. Without scrolling, can you see how to pay you, log in, upload something, or book time? If the answer is no, and you know that tool exists somewhere, you have probably found an easy improvement hiding in plain sight.

If you want a second set of eyes on it, that is the kind of thing I am always happy to look at. No pressure either way. Sometimes just knowing the door is there, and where to put it, is enough to get it done.
